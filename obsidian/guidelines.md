# Template guidelines

Each template should help a distressed person notice what happened, what
matters, and one freely chosen next step—without jargon, blame, or pressure.

The criteria split three ways: the shape a note has to take, the voice it is
written in, and the examples it teaches with. A draft can be checked against
each of the three on its own.

## Shape

1. **One note, one job**

   A template does one of four things: self-clarity, empathy for someone
   else, self-repair, or appreciation. The purpose callout says which, and
   says which sibling template to run instead when this is the wrong one.
   That routing is what a flooded reader most needs, and it is the one thing
   the section headings cannot tell them.

2. **The four questions, adapted—never padded**

   Observation, feeling, need, request. Four sections, or five when a step
   genuinely splits: _Forgive yourself_ separates the needs the action left
   unmet from the need it was reaching for, because mourning and
   self-forgiveness are different work. A step may also be dropped—_Thank
   them_ has no request because there isn't one. Never invent content to fill
   a step out.

3. **Plain question on the surface, term of art behind the fold**

   Headings are questions in ordinary words: _What you feel_, never
   _Feelings_. The NVC term belongs in the callout title, where the reader
   meets it only once the fold is open.

4. **The fold is the unit of teaching**

   A collapsed callout shows nothing but its title, so that one line has to
   be the lesson, written as _X, not Y_—observation not judgment, feelings
   not thoughts, a question not a diagnosis. A title that merely labels the
   section wastes the only line most readers will ever see.

5. **A fixed callout shape**

   Four to six bullets, one short sentence each, in a fixed order: what to
   write, then the tests and the traps, then a not-this/try-this pair last,
   always. The final pair may use its two short sentences; every other bullet
   has one. Every guidance callout in every template ends with one.
   Predictability is the point—a reader who has opened one fold can skim the
   rest.

6. **Works as an Obsidian note**

   `[!note]-` for the purpose callout and `[!info]-` for the section
   guidance; the trailing `-` is what folds them. `#` for the title and `##`
   for the sections, because this arrives as a note of its own rather than as
   part of somebody else's. A feelings section carries an empty feelings
   block and a needs section an empty needs block; a prose section carries
   blank lines to write in. _Italics_ for a line the reader might write or
   think, "double quotes" for words somebody said out loud. The note has to
   stand up without the README and without previous NVC training.

## Voice

7. **Plain-spoken and emotionally safe**

   Write like a calm, trusted person, not a workshop manual. When a term of
   art is genuinely needed, attribute it and translate it in the same breath:
   "Nonviolent Communication calls the two halves mourning and
   self-forgiveness. Mourning is letting yourself feel…" Never shame the
   reader for having feelings, needs, or a messy response.

8. **Accurate NVC distinctions, without jargon traps**

   Observation from evaluation or story, feelings from interpretations of
   another person, needs from one particular person's action, requests from
   demands. A distinction has to survive being said plainly; if it only holds
   in NVC's own vocabulary, it isn't ready to ship.

9. **No covert blame, diagnosis, or mind-reading**

   Do not turn self-judgment into insight. A guess about another person is
   framed as a guess they can correct. NVC is not evidence that someone's
   behaviour was acceptable.

10. **Agency and consent stay intact**

    Requests allow a real no. Repair and appreciation create no obligation.
    The reader can skip a section, pause, or decide not to have the
    conversation at all—and the note says so rather than leaving them to
    assume it.

11. **Useful in the moment**

    A flooded reader can start with one small, concrete answer. Where there
    is a next move, it is something they could actually do—not an abstract
    aspiration, and not a promise never to do it again.

## Examples

12. **One small story, seen from every side**

    The templates run a single ordinary story—two texts that went
    unanswered—through all four, from each side in turn. The recognition does
    real work: it shows that one moment has four honest readings. A new
    template joins that story rather than starting another.

13. **The wrong line is one a real person would write**

    _She ignored me_ earns its place because it is the natural first draft. A
    straw man teaches nothing, and a reader who would never have written it
    learns only that the template is addressed to somebody else. Both halves
    of the pair are the same moment rendered twice, never two different
    situations.

14. **Low stakes, close to home**

    Ordinary domestic friction. Nothing where the honest advice would be to
    talk to someone qualified, and nothing a reader has to have lived through
    a crisis to recognise.

## Across the set

15. **Consistent with the other templates**

    Shared concepts keep the same wording and the same sequence everywhere
    they appear, while each template's own job stays unmistakable.

16. **The README ships with the callouts**

    The README carries a section per template paraphrasing what its folded
    guidance teaches. Change a callout and that paraphrase quietly stops
    being true, so check it in the same pass.
